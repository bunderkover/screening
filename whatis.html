Short, simple explanations, with links to trusted organisations for more. This page is general information, not medical advice. The questionnaires on this site are screening tools, not a diagnosis.

What is ADHD?

ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder) is a difference in how the brain manages attention, activity and impulses. It usually shows up in childhood and often carries on into adult life.

  • Inattention: losing focus, forgetting things, losing track of tasks or belongings.
  • Hyperactivity: restlessness, fidgeting, finding it hard to sit still or wait.
  • Impulsivity: acting or speaking before thinking, interrupting.

Everyone has some of these at times. In ADHD they are frequent, show up in more than one place (home, school, work) and get in the way of daily life. Only a qualified clinician can diagnose it.

Coping strategies

Kids

  • Keep a simple, predictable daily routine.
  • Give one clear instruction at a time and praise specific good behaviour.
  • Behavioral therapy for young children ↗ CHADD

Teenagers

  • Agree a few clear house rules together, with consequences set in advance.
  • Use planners, phone reminders and checklists for school work.
  • Parenting teens with ADHD ↗ CHADD
  • ADHD: what you need to know (children and teens) ↗ NIMH

Adults

  • Break big tasks into small steps and use lists, calendars and reminders.
  • Ask about workplace adjustments, such as a quieter space or written instructions.
  • ADHD in adults: 4 things to know ↗ NIMH
  • ADHD reasonable adjustments ↗ ADHD UK

What is autism?

Autism is a lifelong difference in how a person experiences and interacts with the world. It is not an illness, and every autistic person is different. Some need little support, others need a lot.

  • Communication and social life: finding other people's intentions, small talk or unwritten social rules hard to read.
  • Routines and interests: a strong preference for routine and predictability, and deep, focused interests.
  • Senses: being over- or under-sensitive to things like noise, light, touch or taste.

Many autistic people, especially girls and women, learn to hide or "mask" these differences, which can be tiring. Only a qualified clinician can diagnose autism.

Coping strategies

Kids

  • Prepare them for changes ahead of time, for example with pictures or a visual timetable.
  • Notice what sensory things upset or calm them and adjust where you can.
  • Supporting an autistic child ↗ NHS
  • Meltdowns: a guide for all audiences ↗ National Autistic Society

Teenagers

  • Plan big changes, like a new school, step by step and visit in advance.
  • Make time to rest and recharge, especially if they mask a lot at school.
  • Transition tips ↗ National Autistic Society
  • Masking ↗ National Autistic Society

Adults

  • Build quiet recovery time into busy days.
  • You can ask an employer for reasonable adjustments, with or without a formal diagnosis.
  • Autism and adult life ↗ NHS
  • Employment ↗ National Autistic Society
  • Anxiety ↗ National Autistic Society

Tips are short summaries of the linked pages. If you're worried about yourself or your child, talk to your GP (médecin traitant). ADHD and autism can occur together, and other things like anxiety, low mood or poor sleep can look similar.
